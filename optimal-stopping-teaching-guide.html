I.

TL;DR of the chapter

Optimal stopping is the math of deciding when to stop looking and commit — when your options arrive one at a time, you must decide on the spot, and (usually) can't go back.

  • The book's frame is the secretary problem: interview candidates one by one, decide immediately, no recall.
  • The solution is the Look-Then-Leap Rule: spend the first slice of your options purely calibrating — reject everyone, no matter how good — then commit to the very first person after that who beats everyone you've seen so far.
  • The size of that slice is 37% (mathematically, 1/e). It holds whether the pool is 10 people or a million. Your odds of landing the single best option also converge to 37% — the puzzle doesn't get harder as the pool grows, as long as you know the rule.
  • Change the assumptions, and the shape survives but the number moves:
    • Rejection possible (they might say no) → start proposing sooner (~25% in).
    • Recall allowed (you can go back) → look longer (~61%), with a fallback plan.
    • Full information (you know real scores — a price, a percentile) → the look-phase disappears entirely. Use a Threshold Rule instead: accept anyone above a bar that gets more lenient as your remaining options shrink. Odds jump from 37% to 58%.
  • The same shape reappears everywhere: selling a house (never look back — it's a sunk cost), parking (leap sooner in emptier lots), quitting a winning streak (optimal tries ≈ odds of success ÷ odds of getting caught).
  • Real people stop earlier than 37% — studies land around 25–31%. Not because we're bad at math: searching has a real, felt cost the classical puzzle doesn't price in. Getting impatient isn't irrational, it's just hard to model.
"The therapist tells them to find the right, comfortable balance between impulsivity and overthinking. The algorithm tells them the balance is thirty-seven percent." — Brian Christian & Tom Griffiths, Algorithms to Live By

III.

Facilitation brief — what to say, and when

Before anyone plays

State only the rules — see one option at a time, decide now, no revisiting a rejected one. Don't say "algorithm," "37%," or "optimal" yet. Let the tension between too-eager and too-picky show up as a felt problem before it gets a name.

While they play

  • Have each student write down their personal rule before comparing notes — "I always took the 3rd one," "I waited for anything above 80." This is what makes the reveal land: they're checking their own instinct against the math, not just receiving it.
  • Rotate in small groups (3–4 students), ~8–10 minutes per skin, Rishta → Secretary → Rent Hunt. Fast finishers replay a skin for a better score instead of sitting idle — a built-in pacing device for mixed-speed rooms.
  • Keep the password unrevealed through every rotation. No group should be ahead of another on the reveal.

The reveal moment

Before typing the password, collect a few personal rules on the board and pattern-match them against "look at roughly a third first." Then unlock:

IV.

A two-day flow

Same rhythm as a normal teaching day — three blocks, split by a coffee break and lunch — built around feeling the problem on Day 1 and owning the math on Day 2.

Domain prompts for the closing presentations

Each keeps the same question alive: is this a look-then-leap game, or a threshold game — and why?

  • eMobility — when should a delivery rider stop circling for a closer charging bay?
  • Life sciences — when should a clinical trial stop enrolling and lock its results?
  • Dating apps — swipe culture vs. the classical secretary problem: does infinite recall break the math?
  • Cricket auctions — an IPL franchise bidding against a fixed purse, one player at a time.
  • Organ allocation — a patient deciding whether to accept an offered organ or wait for a better match.
  • Startup exits — a founder fielding acquisition offers with a ticking cap-table clock.
V.

Making it land for everyone

  • Mixed pace: the rotation-with-replay structure already absorbs speed differences — fast players replay for a better score, not new content, so nobody's idle and nobody's rushed.
  • Quieter students: writing down a personal rule before any group talk means everyone has already "spoken" before speaking is required.
  • Multiple entry points: the same idea hits kinesthetic (unplugged cards), visual (the chart), symbolic (the code), and narrative (Kepler's eleven courtships, Michael Trick's proposal) learners at least once each.
  • Discovery over lookup: the password gate isn't just pacing — it enforces "figure it out before you're told," in keeping with wanting less AI-assisted shortcut-taking and more of the class actually deriving the rule.
  • One clean formative check before Day 2 starts: exit ticket — "In one sentence, what should you never do in this game, and why?" (Answer: never leap inside the look phase; never revisit a rejected option.)
